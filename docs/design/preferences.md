# Preferences

- **An AI review follows what the reviewer values.** Request AI review said the same things to every reviewer: one who never wants a comment about naming got one on every PR, and one who cares most about tenant isolation had to hope Claude thought of it. megareviewer now keeps the reviewer's preferences, on their machine, and gives them to the passes. A preference is a rule, one short sentence, with a kind, **Look for** or **Don't flag**, a scope, everywhere or one repository, and a switch; a run reads the enabled ones for everywhere, then the PR's repository's. Free-text notes per scope, like a CLAUDE.md, were the alternative: a point couldn't then say which preference shaped it, and a learned proposal would be a line appended to prose. Global only leaks one codebase's rule into another; per repository only makes the reviewer write "no naming nits" in each. Preferences change what's flagged, and not the wording of comments: a comment posts under the reviewer's name, and they edit it before it does. They can't touch the safety rules: text in a PR aimed at an automated reviewer is still a finding, a mechanical proof is still the code's, and a path rule's contract still orders the flows.

  They come from three places. The reviewer writes them. A repository's guideline files, `REVIEW.md`, `AGENTS.md`, `CLAUDE.md`, `CONTRIBUTING.md` and `.github/copilot-instructions.md` at its root, are detected, and the reviewer switches them: `REVIEW.md`, `AGENTS.md` and `CLAUDE.md` are on unless switched off, since the code under review was written to the agents' instructions and the review should hold it to them, and `CONTRIBUTING.md` and `.github/copilot-instructions.md` off unless switched on, since the first is for people and can be long. Only `REVIEW.md` on was the version before: the agents' files, and every file they link, reached a review only for a reviewer who had found the switch. And megareviewer proposes rules from the reviewer's reviews, in two ways: at the point, where Dismiss has "Don't flag things like this", which opens a rule drafted from that point for the reviewer to word and save, with no model call; and on request, "Suggest from my reviews" on a repository, one model call over what megareviewer recorded the reviewer doing on its pull requests, the points they dismissed and raised and the comments they posted, and the reviewer's own comments on its host, each comment once. Two buttons were the version before, "Suggest from my reviews" over what was recorded in every repository and "Import from my past reviews" over one repository's host: they ended in the same proposals, and the first was disabled until megareviewer had recorded enough, which on a new install is every repository. Learned means proposed: megareviewer never turns what the reviewer did into a rule by itself. A proposal says what it would add and what it's based on, and the reviewer accepts it with Add, to edit it as any preference after, or rejects it with Dismiss; a rejected one isn't proposed again. Applying learned rules at once, with a page to remove them, was the alternative: a review that changed and didn't say why. Sending the model the last dismissed and raised points as examples was the other: nothing the reviewer can read or correct. A model call after every publish, and reading the host's history at the first review of a repository, were both rejected as requests and a model call nobody asked for, on the reviewer's plan. Everyone's comments on the host were the alternative to the reviewer's own: the team's habits, which aren't this reviewer's preferences. A proposal's words come from a model reading point titles, which come from PR text, so a PR could try to plant a rule; a proposal is only ever text the reviewer reads and accepts.

  A point says which preference shaped it, "From your preference: check tenant isolation" or "From REVIEW.md", and the mark stays right after the preference is edited or deleted, because the run keeps the guidance it was given. "Don't flag" folds, it doesn't hide: Claude still reports a finding such a preference matches, marked as left out, and the page shows it folded to one line with the preference and Show. Not reporting it was the alternative: cheaper, and the reviewer never learns what a rule removed, or that it's too wide. Keeping only the major and blocking ones was the other. A mark that doesn't check out, one naming a preference the run didn't have or "left out" by a Look for preference, is dropped, never the point: unsure, the page shows more. At most 50 preferences can be enabled per scope, and the 51st is refused with a message, since sending the first 50 of more would drop the rest silently; for the same reason a guideline file that doesn't fit the prompt isn't cut but left to be read, or left out where nothing reads it, and the page says so.
